Langevin diffusion

Created on November 14, 2026

2026   ·   langevin   ·   math

Recall that a stochastic process $X_t$ is called a diffusion process if it satisfies

\[\begin{aligned} dX_t = \mu(t, X_t)dt + \sigma(t, X_t)dW_t. \end{aligned}\]

In other words, from the Markov property, the movement of the stochastic process only depends on the time $t$ and its current location. In particular, we study time-homogeneous processes, in which the functions $\mu$ and $\sigma$ do not depend on $t$ [Chewi, Log-concave sampling].

Definition. Let $V: \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R$ be a potential in $C^2$ and strongly convex function. The Langevin diffusion is the stochastic process taking values in $\mathbb R^d$ and satisfies

\[\begin{aligned} dX_t = -\nabla V(X_t)dt + \sqrt{2}dW_t, \end{aligned}\]

where $B_t$ is the Brownian motion.

Then, we already have that $X_0 \sim \rho_0$ is a random variable distributed according to $\rho_0$, then we calculate the infinitesimal generator of the Langevin Diffusion. Firstly, apply the Itô formula, we can rewrite the Langevin diffusion as:

\[\begin{aligned} f(X_t) - f(X_0) = \int_0^t -\langle \nabla f(X_s), \nabla V(X_s) \rangle + \mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(X_s))ds + \sqrt{2}\int_0^t \langle \nabla f(X_s), dW_s \rangle. \end{aligned}\]

Since $W_t$ is the Brownian motion in $\mathbb R^d$, hence $dW_t$ is mean-zero. Moreover, it is also independent from $X_t$ and $\nabla f(X_t)$, then taking the expected value, we have

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E [\langle \nabla f(X_s), dW_s \rangle] = 0, \end{aligned}\]

or

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E \left[ \sqrt{2}\int_0^t\langle \nabla f(X_s), dW_s \rangle \right] = 0. \end{aligned}\]

Write down the remaining term as Taylor expansion, and take the expected value of both side, we also have

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(X_t) | X_0 = x] - f(x) &= t[-\langle \nabla f(x), \nabla V(x) \rangle + \mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(x))] + o(t) \\ \frac{P_t f(x) - f(x)}{t}& = -\langle \nabla f(x), \nabla V(x) \rangle + \Delta f(x) + \frac{o(t)}{t}, \end{aligned}\]

When $t \to 0$, we have

\[\begin{aligned} Lf(x) = \lim_{t\searrow 0} \frac{P_t f(x) - f(x)}{t} = -\langle \nabla f(x), \nabla V(x) \rangle + \Delta f(x). \end{aligned}\]

Moreover, from the chain rule, we also have

\[\begin{aligned} df(X_t) = Lf(X_t)dt + \sqrt{2}\nabla f(X_t) dW_t. \end{aligned}\]

Take the expectation of both sides, we have

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[f(X_t)] = \mathbb E[Lf(X_t)]. \end{aligned}\]

As $\rho_t$ has the law of $X_t$, then

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(X_t)] = \int_{\mathbb R^d} f(x)\rho_t(x)dx. \end{aligned}\]

Therefore,

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathbb R^d} f(x)\rho_t(x)dx &= \int_{\mathbb R^d} Lf(x)\rho_t(x)dx \\ &= \int_{\mathbb R^d} [\Delta f(x) -\langle \nabla f(x), \nabla V(x) \rangle ] \rho_t(x)dx. \end{aligned}\]

From the divergence theorem, we imply that

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla f\cdot\nabla V\rho_t = \nabla f \cdot (\rho_t\nabla V) = -f \nabla \cdot (\rho_t \nabla V), \end{aligned}\]

and we can rewrite as

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\int f\rho_t = \int f (\Delta \rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho_t \nabla V)). \end{aligned}\]

Using the definition of weak derivative, we imply that

\[\begin{aligned} \partial_t \rho_t = \Delta \rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho_t \nabla V) = \mathrm{div}((\nabla \log\rho_t + \nabla V) \rho_t), \end{aligned}\]

where $\Delta$ denotes the Laplacian operator (diffusion) and the divergence operator as the drift, and the $\log\rho_t$ is called the score of $\rho_t$. Furthermore, the continuity equation can also be described through a flow model on $\mathbb R^d$.
